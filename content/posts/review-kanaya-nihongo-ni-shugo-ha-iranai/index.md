This was an interesting book. The author Kanaya is a Japanese linguist and at the time of writing he had been teaching Japanese in University of Montreal for 13 years. So he knows a lot about French and English as well. In this book, he shows a lot of examples illustrating the grammatical difference between the three languages throughout the book. It uses pretty plain language and avoids using too much jargon, making it pretty easy for laymen like me to understand.

In Kanaya's view, the grammar system of Japanese that is widely used today in both domestic and foreign classes (which he calls 学校文法 = "school grammar") is a flawed product from linguists trying to shoehorn the language into the grammatical framework for European languages, and it has been seriously undermining Japanese language education for more than a century. As a teacher who has been having to teach Japanese based on this broken system, he says he hopes this book, which points out its major issues, will "amend his sin" ("罪滅ぼし").

The main problem of the system is that 学校文法 frames a sentence as essentially a subject and a predicate. He believes Japanese doesn't need subjects, as the book title says. This isn't just to say subjects can be omitted in Japanese. He's claiming the grammar system of the language doesn't need the concept of subject itself. And he isn't the first person to say this; It was first proposed by Mikami, and Kanaya fully supports it. (He seems to almost worship him.)

What I found more interesting was some of the other claims he makes. For one, he says Japanese doesn't have 人称代名詞 (pronouns), at least like those in English and French (like "I" and "you"). Pronouns in English and French behave differently from normal nouns; they have special forms like "my", "her", "mine", "yourself", etc.; they also affect word order, in English by not allowing a phrase like "to give him it" (while "to give him a key" is valid); French even puts a pronoun object *before* the verb. Japanese "pronouns", on the other hand, are merely nouns, he says, because they have none of these special properties and they behave just like any other nouns in terms of syntax. Not only that, there's no fixed set of "pronouns" because you can use パパ, 兄さん, your own name, etc. in place of 私 (without affecting the rest of the sentence). This was a new perspective to me and I agree Japanese pronouns are very different from those in European languages but I think it's still useful to call them pronouns.

And yes, he hates あなた, by the way. In his classes he even tells his students to cross out あなた from example sentences in the textbook.

What I found most interesting in the book was the claim that there's this spectrum of artificial (人為) versus natural (自然) where passive, causative, transitive and intransitive forms all fall into. He says the passive form れる/られる (which was originally る/らる) comes from ある, and the causative form せる/させる (originally す/さす) from す(る), and that ある represents being as it is, thus natural whereas する artificial. I don't know if this is a well supported theory but it makes sense.

And it doesn't stop there; he reveals something mind blowing about transitive/intransitive verb pairs. Those pairs include 立ち/立て and 切り/切れ (we're looking at masu-stem form for convenience's sake). So there seems to be this -i vs -e dichotomy. Then he says that the godan verbs (-i) are the default versions of the verbs. Some of which are intransitive (立ち), others transitive (切り). Then people created alternative forms by turning -i into -e sound, so that -e acts as a ["mark"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markedness) to show, "Hey, this is the unusual version". What's unusual about it? Think how often you perform the actions of 立つ vs 立てる. 立つ feels like a more common, frequent thing to do. It feels like a more primitive action than making something stand. So 立ち was the original, intransitive mode, from which 立て was derived as the altered mode. And the exact opposite is the case with verbs like 切る, where transitive (cutting something) is the normal mode, and 切れる is more unusual.

So it turns out "i" is more basic than "e" semantically... What if it was also the case phonetically...? It actually is!!! He says "e" is the result of combining "i" and "a"! So people created 立て by adding "a" after 立ち, which then caused the "i" and "a" to merge into "e". Likewise, 嘆き (なげき; to mourn) comes from 長息 (ながいき; long breath/sigh) with が and い fused into げ. It turns out Japanese used to not have the "e" sound at all! Oh my god. This was mind-blowing to know. 

I should note a lot of claims in the book seemed to lack scientific evidence and rely on whether it feels intuitive and clean (he keeps mentioning Ockham's razor throughout the book). He mentions this book targets the general public so that may be why, but I wonder how his theories are regarded in the academic world. Nevertheless, this book was quite an interesting read and I'd like to check out his other books some time. Apparently this was his first book.
