This was an interesting book. Kanaya, a Japanese linguist, had been teaching Japanese at the University of Montreal for 13 years at the time of writing. So, he knows a lot about both French and English grammar. In his book, he shows a lot of examples of the grammatical differences between the three languages. It uses plain language and avoids using too much jargon, making it pretty easy for laymen like me to understand.

Kanaya thinks the modern Japanese grammar system that is widely used in both domestic and foreign classes (which he calls 学校文法 = "school grammar") is a flawed product, arising from linguists trying to shoehorn the language into the grammatical framework of European languages, which has been seriously undermining Japanese language education for more than a century. As a teacher who had to teach Japanese based on this broken system, he hopes that pointing out its major issues will "amend his sin" ("罪滅ぼし").

The main problem of the system is that 学校文法 frames a sentence as essentially a subject and a predicate. But he believes Japanese doesn't need subjects, as the book title says. This isn't just to say subjects can be omitted in Japanese. He claims the grammar system of the language doesn't need the concept of a subject at all. And he isn't the first person to say this; it was first proposed by Mikami, a high school math teacher turned linguist, in 1960. Kanaya fully supports it (and seems to almost worship him).

What I found more interesting was some of the other claims he makes. For one, he says Japanese doesn't have 人称代名詞 (pronouns), at least like those in English and French (like "I" and "you"). Pronouns in English and French behave differently from normal nouns; they have special forms like "her," "mine," "yourself," etc. They also affect word order in English by not allowing a phrase like "to give him it" (while "to give him a key" is valid). French even puts a pronoun object *before* the verb. But according to Kanaya, Japanese "pronouns" are merely nouns because they have none of these special properties and they behave just like any other nouns in terms of syntax. Not only that, there's no fixed set of "pronouns" because you can use パパ, 兄さん, your own name, etc., in place of 私 without affecting the rest of the sentence. This was a new perspective to me. I agree Japanese pronouns are very different from those in European languages, but I think it's still useful to call them pronouns.

And yes, he hates あなた, by the way. In his classes he even tells his students to cross out あなた from example sentences in the textbook.

What I found most interesting was the claim that there's this spectrum of artificial (人為) versus natural (自然) verbs in which passive, causative, transitive and intransitive forms all belong. He says the passive form れる/られる (which was originally る/らる) comes from ある, and the causative form せる/させる (originally す/さす) from す(る), and that ある represents being "as it is" and natural, whereas する is artificial. I don't know if this is a well-supported theory, but it makes sense.

And it doesn't stop there: he reveals something mind blowing about transitive/intransitive verb pairs. Those pairs include 立ち/立て and 切り/切れ (we're looking at masu-stem form for convenience's sake). So there seems to be a *-i* vs *-e* dichotomy. He claims that the godan verbs (*-i*) are the default versions of the verbs, some of which are intransitive (立ち), and others transitive (切り). Then people created alternative forms of these verbs by turning the *-i* sound into an *-e* sound, so that *-e* acts as a ["mark"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markedness) to show this is the unusual version. What's unusual about it? Think how often you perform the actions of 立つ vs 立てる. 立つ feels like a more common, frequent thing to do. It feels like a more primitive action than making something else stand. So 立ち was the original, intransitive mode, from which the transitive 立て form was derived. And the exact opposite is the case with verbs like 切る, where transitive (cutting something) is the normal mode, and 切れる is more unusual.

So it turns out *-i* is more basic than *-e* semantically... What if it was also the case phonetically...? It actually is!!! He says *-e* is the result of combining *-i* and *-a*! So people created 立て by adding *-a* after 立ち, which then caused the *-i* and *-a* to merge into *-e*. Likewise, 嘆き (なげき, to mourn) comes from 長息 (ながいき, long breath/sigh) with が and い fused into げ. It turns out Japanese used to not have the *-e* sound at all! Oh my god. This was mind-blowing to learn. 

I should note a lot of his claims seemed to lack scientific evidence and rely on whether it feels intuitive and clean (he frequently mentions Occam's razor throughout the book). He mentions this book targets the general public so that may be why, but I wonder how his theories are regarded in the academic world. Nevertheless, this book was quite an interesting read and I'd like to check out his other books some time. Apparently this was his first book.
